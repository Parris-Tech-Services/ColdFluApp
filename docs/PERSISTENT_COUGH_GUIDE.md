# Persistent cough after a respiratory infection

**Status:** public research summary, not clinically reviewed medical advice  
**Updated:** 15 September 2026  
**Scope:** adults with a mainly dry cough that began with, or followed, a respiratory infection. This page does not diagnose the cause of an individual cough.

> A lingering cough after a respiratory infection can be extremely annoying even when the original infection has largely settled. The most common postinfectious pattern lasts about **3–8 weeks**. The practical goal is usually to reduce irritation, look for clues to another treatable cause, and know when the pattern needs medical review.

## How long can it last?

A cough persisting for roughly **3–8 weeks** after a respiratory infection is commonly described as **postinfectious (postviral) cough** once other plausible causes and red flags have been considered. A 2024 review estimated that this affects about **11–25% of adults after respiratory infection**.

The cough is thought to reflect a mixture of residual airway inflammation, temporarily increased cough-reflex sensitivity, mucus changes and temporarily impaired mucus clearance. Most uncomplicated cases improve with time, but there is no reliable way to predict the exact day an individual cough will stop.

If a cough reaches **8 weeks**, Australian chronic-cough guidance recommends moving from simple self-care to a structured assessment, usually including a focused history/examination, chest X-ray and spirometry.

## Why might it be worse in the morning and at night?

Morning/night timing does not identify the cause by itself, but several common contributors can become more noticeable then:

- **Postinfectious cough hypersensitivity:** a temporarily over-sensitive cough reflex can be triggered by talking, cold air, a throat tickle, dry air, strong smells or repeated coughing itself.
- **Rhinitis or post-nasal symptoms:** nasal discharge, congestion, sneezing, allergies or sinus symptoms can irritate the upper airway, especially after lying down.
- **Reflux:** cough can worsen after evening meals or lying down. Reflux is more plausible when heartburn, regurgitation or a meal/position relationship is present; night cough alone does not prove reflux.
- **Asthma or post-viral airway reactivity:** cough can be prominent at night and may occur with wheeze, chest tightness, breathlessness, exercise/cold-air triggers or a history of asthma/allergy. Cough-variant asthma can occur without obvious wheeze and may need objective testing.
- **Environmental irritation:** smoke, vaping, dust, strong cleaning sprays, fragrances, aerosols, mould, very dry air or excessive humidity can aggravate a sensitive airway.

## What to do when the cough starts

For a **dry, tickly cough**, low-risk behavioural cough-control strategies can reduce repeated bouts even though the strongest evidence comes from chronic refractory cough rather than short postviral cough.

1. Try not to take a large preparatory gasp.
2. Close your mouth and **swallow once or twice**.
3. Take a small sip of water or use a plain lozenge/hard sweet.
4. Breathe gently through your nose.
5. Use a slow, longer exhalation through lightly pursed lips for about 30–60 seconds.
6. If a cough is unavoidable, aim for one gentle, purposeful cough rather than a long run of hard coughs or repeated throat clearing.

Do **not** deliberately suppress a cough that is needed to clear significant sputum, blood or an aspirated object.

## Simple things worth trying

### Honey, water and lozenges

Honey, ordinary hydration and lozenges can provide short-term throat comfort. Evidence is stronger for acute cough than for a several-week adult postinfectious cough, so these should be viewed as **comfort measures**, not treatments proven to shorten the illness.

### Saline if the nose is involved

If you also have nasal congestion, watery discharge, sneezing, allergy symptoms or a post-nasal sensation, saline spray or irrigation is a reasonable low-risk option. Saline may improve rhinitis symptoms, although direct evidence that it stops a postinfectious cough is weak.

For nasal irrigation, use water prepared exactly as recommended by the product or public-health guidance; do not use untreated tap water in a rinse device.

### Do not assume more humidity is better

A warm shower can feel soothing, but steam has not been shown to reliably shorten a respiratory cough. Indoor humidity around **30–50%** is generally a sensible target. Excess humidity can promote dust mites and mould.

If you are running a humidifier every night without knowing whether the room is actually dry, a short **humidifier-off trial** while measuring room humidity is a reasonable experiment. If a humidifier is used, keep it clean and follow the manufacturer's water/maintenance instructions.

### Remove airway irritants

For several days, minimise smoke, vaping exposure, fragrance diffusers, strong cleaning sprays, aerosols and dusty tasks. This is particularly useful when the cough is triggered by smells, cold air or throat irritation.

### If reflux seems plausible

A low-risk trial is to:

- finish food at least **3 hours before bedtime**;
- avoid lying flat immediately after eating;
- consider head-of-bed elevation when reflux symptoms are present.

Do not assume an isolated cough needs acid-suppressing medication. CHEST and Australian cough guidance advise against routine acid suppression when there is no history or objective evidence of reflux.

## What usually does not shorten an uncomplicated postinfectious cough

Current evidence does **not** support automatically adding antibiotics, inhaled corticosteroids, bronchodilators, PPIs or multiple OTC cough medicines for every lingering postviral cough.

That does not mean these medicines are never useful. They can be appropriate when there is evidence for a specific cause such as asthma, allergic rhinitis, reflux or pertussis. The important distinction is **cause-directed treatment rather than treating cough duration alone**.

Dextromethorphan may temporarily suppress some dry coughs, but evidence is mixed and it has not been shown to shorten postinfectious cough. Medicine interactions matter, so a pharmacist should check suitability if you are considering it, especially if you take antidepressants or other serotonergic medicines.

## A practical 7-day experiment

### Days 1–3

- Keep the room around 30–50% relative humidity rather than making it as humid as possible.
- Pause unnecessary humidifier use and remove fragrance/aerosol irritants.
- Use normal hydration, honey or lozenges for comfort if helpful.
- Use the swallow/sip/nasal-breathing cough-control sequence at the first tickle.
- Finish evening food at least 3 hours before bed.
- Record morning and night cough severity from 0–10 plus sleep awakenings.

### Days 4–7

- Continue the same approach if the trend is improving.
- If nasal symptoms are prominent, add saline and consider pharmacist/GP-guided rhinitis treatment rather than stacking cough syrups.
- If reflux symptoms are prominent, discuss symptom-directed reflux treatment with a pharmacist or GP.
- If there is wheeze, chest tightness, breathlessness, exercise/cold-air triggering or a history suggesting asthma, ask a GP about objective airway assessment rather than self-starting inhalers.

## When to book a GP

A cough already lasting several weeks is reasonable to discuss with a GP if it is **not clearly improving**, is repeatedly disrupting sleep, is diagnostically unclear or has an unusual pattern.

Earlier assessment is particularly useful if there are:

- coughing fits with an inspiratory whoop or vomiting after coughing, which can suggest pertussis;
- wheeze, chest tightness or breathlessness;
- a substantial productive cough rather than an isolated dry cough;
- recurrent episodes or significant smoke/vape/occupational exposure;
- persistent voice change or swallowing difficulty.

If the cough reaches **8 weeks**, Australian guidance supports chronic-cough assessment including chest X-ray and spirometry, with further cause-directed testing when appropriate.

## Seek urgent medical help for red flags

Prompt assessment is needed for coughing blood, significant or worsening shortness of breath, persistent fever/systemic illness, unexplained weight loss, swallowing difficulty, persistent hoarseness, recurrent pneumonia, substantial persistent productive cough, an abnormal respiratory examination, or deterioration rather than gradual improvement.

Call **Triple Zero (000)** for severe breathing difficulty, blue/grey colour, collapse, severe chest pain or another acute emergency.
