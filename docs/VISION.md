# Vision and principles

## Vision

Help people make safer, better-informed health decisions by explaining what may help, what probably does not, what may cause harm, what the evidence actually supports, and when professional or emergency care is needed.

The project began with common respiratory illness. That remains the first controlled content collection, but the long-term vision is a broad Australian-first health reference spanning first aid, family health, medicines, heart/circulation, sleep, movement, nutrition, mental health, neurodivergence, pregnancy/breastfeeding, chronic illness, aged care, workplace/environmental health and digital-health literacy.

## The problem

Health advice is fragmented across agencies, clinical guidelines, papers, professional education, product marketing, traditional practice, podcasts, social media and lived experience. The same intervention can have different evidence for symptom relief, prevention, disease modification, quality of life or mortality. Medicine availability, emergency pathways, vaccination programmes, workplace duties and service access also vary by jurisdiction.

People commonly need three different things, which should not be blurred together:

1. **What do I do right now?** — first aid, red flags and escalation.
2. **What does the evidence say?** — treatments, harms, tests, prevention and uncertainty.
3. **How can I learn more?** — trustworthy podcasts, videos, guidelines, studies and quizzes.

The project will turn fragmented material into small, traceable, independently reviewable modules and learning resources. It is not intended to diagnose a user or replace a clinician.

## Core user promise

For every material claim, a reader should be able to understand:

- what outcome the intervention/test/risk factor is claimed to affect;
- how confident the evidence is and why;
- whether the benefit is symptom relief, prevention, disease modification, comfort, diagnostic information or another outcome;
- what practical use was actually studied;
- who should avoid it or seek individual advice;
- which jurisdiction and population the advice applies to;
- where the information came from and when it was reviewed;
- whether the source is a guideline, systematic review, trial, regulator, observational study or lived-experience resource.

## Product principles

1. **Safety outranks comprehensiveness.** A broad roadmap does not justify publishing unreviewed instructions.
2. **Evidence is claim-specific.** A medicine, diet, breathing practice, supplement or test does not receive one permanent rating; each claimed outcome is rated separately.
3. **Uncertainty is content.** Conflicting, sparse, indirect or low-quality evidence is shown plainly.
4. **Comfort is not cure.** Symptom comfort, physiological effects, disease duration, disease modification, complication prevention and transmission reduction remain distinct.
5. **Local rules are explicit.** Australian information is the first jurisdictional layer, never an unlabelled global default.
6. **Triage is prominent.** Emergency warnings and vulnerable-population advice must be reachable without reading a long article, taking a quiz or watching media.
7. **Sources are inspectable.** References link to the exact guideline, review, paper or regulatory record — not merely an organisation homepage where an exact source exists.
8. **Freshness is visible.** Every published module has review dates, owners and triggers for early reassessment.
9. **AI drafts; accountable humans approve.** Clinical and editorial approval cannot be automated away.
10. **Accessibility is part of correctness.** Plain language, semantic structure, keyboard support, readable contrast and low-bandwidth operation are baseline requirements.
11. **Personal data stays separate from public evidence.** A public reference should not become a public health record.
12. **Learning media is supplementary.** Podcasts, videos and quizzes support understanding but never outrank emergency instructions or reviewed evidence.
13. **Lived experience matters without becoming efficacy evidence.** Patient/carer stories can teach what care feels like and what matters, but do not prove that a treatment works.
14. **Professional use needs boundaries.** Nursing/aged-care/first-aid material supports learning; local policy, scope of practice, care plans and current professional guidelines remain authoritative.

## Audience

Primary audiences:

- people managing common health questions at home;
- parents and carers deciding what to do next;
- trained workplace/community first aiders needing a reliable refresher;
- people living with chronic conditions who want understandable evidence and trustworthy resources;
- nurses, aged-care workers and other health workers using it as an educational reference, not a clinical decision-support system.

Secondary audiences include pharmacists, clinicians, educators, researchers and students who want a concise evidence trail.

## Global usefulness

The core evidence layer can serve readers worldwide. Local overlays will provide country-specific medicine names/scheduling, emergency numbers, vaccination programmes, care pathways, workplace rules, units and regulator guidance. A country is not “supported” until its overlay has accountable review and a freshness policy.

## Linked health tools

The programme may link to specialised companion apps while maintaining clear boundaries:

- **HealthLens** for private personal health-data analysis and trends;
- **HeartCalm** for calm/palpitation episode support, logging and escalation guardrails;
- **ColdFluApp / Health Reference** for public evidence and learning.

Cross-linking does not mean automatic transfer of health data or shared clinical status.

## Non-goals

- Diagnosing an individual illness or estimating personal risk from symptoms alone.
- Replacing emergency services, a doctor, nurse, pharmacist, poison-information service or local workplace/clinical policy.
- Recommending brands, selling supplements, accepting paid rankings or embedding sponsored clinical rankings.
- Hosting unmoderated user-generated treatment advice in the initial product.
- Collecting personal symptom/health data in the public reference product.
- Publishing dosing calculators without separate clinical validation and risk review.
- Treating podcasts/videos/quizzes as proof of clinical competence.
- Copying identifiable personal/family health histories from private conversations or uploads into public repository content.

## Success

Early success means the content pipeline reliably prevents incomplete or unreviewed modules from publishing.

Longer-term product success means users can:

- find the right level of care quickly;
- distinguish emergency, urgent, routine and self-care situations;
- understand the confidence and limitations of health claims;
- navigate from a practical summary to the exact source;
- learn through accessible audio/video/quiz options;
- find family, first-aid, nursing/aged-care and chronic-condition resources without unsafe information blending;
- move to companion personal-data tools without exposing private health information in the public reference.

Success should be measured through comprehension, accessibility, source freshness, correction time, search usefulness and safety — not engagement for its own sake.
