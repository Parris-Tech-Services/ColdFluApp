# Postinfectious dry cough — evidence synthesis

> **Status:** AI-assisted discovery draft. Not clinically reviewed, not approved for publication, and not medical advice.
>
> **Reviewed against sources through:** 15 September 2026

## Executive synthesis

A predominantly dry cough that persists after an acute respiratory infection and lasts about 3–8 weeks is commonly labelled **postinfectious (postviral) cough** once other plausible causes and red flags have been considered. A 2024 review estimates it affects about **11–25% of adults after respiratory infection**. The proposed mechanisms include residual airway inflammation, increased bronchial/cough-reflex sensitivity, mucus changes and temporarily impaired clearance (`cmaj-postinfectious-2024`).

The most important evidence finding is negative: **there is no well-supported medicine that reliably shortens or stops an otherwise uncomplicated postinfectious cough.** Reviews of inhaled corticosteroids, bronchodilators and oral agents have not shown convincing benefit for undifferentiated postinfectious cough (`cmaj-postinfectious-2024`). This does not mean inhalers, rhinitis treatment or reflux treatment never work; it means they should be used when evidence points to a specific treatable condition rather than reflexively given to everyone with a lingering cough.

For adults, Australian CICADA guidance defines chronic cough as **more than 8 weeks**. At that point a focused assessment including **chest X-ray and spirometry** is recommended, followed by cause-directed investigation (`cicada-2024`).

## 1. Natural history and likely mechanisms

### Postinfectious cough

**Evidence confidence: moderate-high for the syndrome and time window; low for exact individual duration.**

- Typical definition: cough persisting **3–8 weeks** after an acute respiratory infection (`cmaj-postinfectious-2024`, `chest-postinfectious-2006`).
- Estimated occurrence: about **11–25% of adults** after respiratory infection (`cmaj-postinfectious-2024`).
- Proposed mechanisms include increased bronchial sensitivity, airway inflammation, altered mucus production and impaired mucociliary clearance (`cmaj-postinfectious-2024`).
- Most improve spontaneously. Persistence beyond 8 weeks should trigger reconsideration of the diagnosis and investigation for chronic-cough causes (`cicada-2024`).

### What is not known well

No robust evidence was found that allows a defensible statement such as “X% are resolved by week 3, Y% by week 4, Z% by week 6 and 8” specifically for adult postinfectious cough. Acute-cough studies show that many coughs last 2–3 weeks or longer, but those cohorts should not be misused to manufacture a postinfectious-cough resolution curve.

## 2. Differential diagnosis: what else can look like postinfectious cough?

### Upper-airway cough syndrome / rhinitis / rhinosinusitis

Clues: nasal blockage, sneezing, itching, watery discharge, postnasal sensation, frequent throat clearing, seasonal/allergen pattern. CICADA recognises chronic rhinosinusitis as a possible chronic-cough contributor. Intranasal corticosteroids are first-line treatment for chronic rhinosinusitis/rhinitis, but evidence that they directly improve cough is weak (`cicada-2024`).

Saline irrigation has low-certainty evidence for improving allergic-rhinitis symptoms and is generally low risk; direct evidence that it stops postinfectious cough is lacking (`cochrane-saline-rhinitis`).

### Asthma, cough-variant asthma and eosinophilic bronchitis

Asthma can present with cough, including without obvious wheeze in cough-variant asthma. In chronic-cough cohorts, eosinophilic airway inflammation is common, but these prevalence data should not be projected directly onto a short-lived postinfectious cough (`cicada-2024`).

Australian Asthma Handbook guidance supports objective testing rather than guessing:

- spirometry with bronchodilator responsiveness;
- FeNO when available;
- repeat testing or bronchial provocation if suspicion remains despite normal initial results (`asthma-handbook-diagnosis`).

A normal spirometry result does not completely exclude asthma. However, absent wheeze or dyspnoea and a clear post-infection onset make blind bronchodilator treatment less compelling than cause-directed assessment if symptoms persist.

### Gastro-oesophageal / airway reflux

Night cough can coexist with reflux, but timing alone is not diagnostic. CHEST recommends avoiding meals within **3 hours of bedtime** and using head-of-bed elevation as part of management when reflux-cough is plausible (`chest-reflux-cough-2016`).

Crucially, **PPI therapy alone is unlikely to resolve cough in people without heartburn or regurgitation** and is recommended against as an empiric cough treatment in that group (`chest-reflux-cough-2016`). Australian CICADA similarly recommends against acid suppression without a history or objective evidence of reflux (`cicada-2024`).

### Laryngeal/cough hypersensitivity

CICADA describes cough hypersensitivity as excessive coughing triggered by low-level thermal, mechanical or chemical stimuli. Laryngeal dysfunction and hypersensitivity can be associated with chronic cough, and speech pathology including breathing/cough-control techniques has supportive evidence (`cicada-2024`).

Postinfectious cough plausibly overlaps with temporary cough-reflex hypersensitivity, but most speech-pathology evidence is in **chronic refractory/unexplained cough**, not 3–8 week postviral cough.

### Pertussis

Consider pertussis when cough is paroxysmal and accompanied by **post-tussive vomiting and/or an inspiratory whoop**, or when epidemiological exposure makes it plausible (`cmaj-postinfectious-2024`, `chest-postinfectious-2006`). Antibiotics are not routine treatment for an ordinary postviral cough; early pertussis is an exception because diagnosis and treatment matter for transmission and may help if given early.

### Medicines and exposures

Chronic-cough assessment should include:

- ACE inhibitor use;
- smoking/vaping and second-hand smoke;
- occupational/environmental fumes, dust, aerosols and strong odours;
- abnormal chest imaging or systemic disease clues (`cicada-2024`).

## 3. Environmental interventions

### Humidifiers and room humidity

**Evidence confidence: high for humidity targets and environmental risks; low for humidifiers as cough treatment.**

National Asthma Council Australia recommends indoor relative humidity around **30–50%** where possible. High humidity favours dust mites and mould; humidifiers are **not usually recommended for everyday use** unless the environment is genuinely very dry or a clinician has advised them (`nac-humidity-2024`).

This matters because “humid air soothes a cough” is not the same as “running a humidifier every night treats a lingering cough.” If room humidity is already adequate, extra humidity may add no benefit and may increase allergen load.

For ultrasonic humidifiers specifically, the US EPA notes that they can aerosolise **microorganisms and minerals** from the tank. If used, tanks should be emptied/refilled frequently, cleaned carefully, and low-mineral/distilled water reduces mineral aerosol (`epa-humidifiers-2022`).

**Practical evidence-led experiment:** if a humidifier has been used nightly during a persistent dry cough, a 3–7 night **humidifier-off trial** while measuring room RH with a hygrometer is a reasonable low-risk way to test whether it is neutral or aggravating. Aim for 30–50% rather than “as humid as possible.”

### Essential oils / fragrance

Strong odours and chemical irritants can trigger cough in hypersensitive airways. Essential-oil diffusion should not be assumed to help cough. During an environmental trial, removing fragrance/aerosol variables is preferable to adding them.

### Air purifiers / HEPA

HEPA filtration can reduce particulate exposure and has some evidence in dust-mite allergic rhinitis (`hepa-rhinitis-rct-2020`). It is **not a proven treatment for postinfectious cough**. It becomes more reasonable when there is a clear allergy, smoke or particulate trigger rather than as a universal cough purchase.

### Smoke, vaping, cleaning sprays and aerosols

Avoiding respiratory irritants is a standard component of cough management (`cicada-2024`). Temporarily minimise smoke, vaping exposure, strong cleaning sprays, fragrance diffusers and dusty tasks while the cough reflex is sensitive.

## 4. Comfort measures and non-drug symptom relief

### Honey

Honey has limited evidence for short-term cough symptom relief, strongest in acute upper-respiratory cough and paediatric studies, not adult postinfectious cough. It may soothe the throat but there is no good evidence that it **shortens a 3–8 week postinfectious cough** (`nice-acute-cough`).

Therefore honey can be used for comfort if tolerated, but escalating the amount is unlikely to address the underlying mechanism.

### Lozenges / hard sweets / demulcents

Lozenges increase swallowing and may temporarily reduce throat irritation. Strepsils in Australia are labelled for temporary relief of sore-throat/mouth discomfort, not as a treatment that resolves postinfectious cough (`healthdirect-strepsils`).

A plain lozenge or hard sweet can also be used as part of a cough-suppression strategy by encouraging swallowing when the urge to cough starts. Avoid assuming “medicated” automatically means more effective for cough.

### Water and hydration

Adequate hydration is sensible for comfort and laryngeal hygiene, but there is no strong evidence that forcing extra fluid beyond normal hydration shortens postinfectious cough. Small sips of water are useful as a **behavioural interruption** when the cough urge begins.

### Warm versus cold fluids

No high-quality evidence was found showing that one fluid temperature shortens cough duration. Choose the temperature that feels most soothing and does not trigger coughing.

### Steam / hot showers

A Cochrane review found no consistent benefit from heated humidified air for common-cold outcomes (`cochrane-steam-2017`). A warm shower may feel soothing, but should be framed as comfort rather than treatment. Avoid deliberate exposure to very hot steam because benefit is unproven and scald injury is avoidable.

### Staying warm

Comfortable warmth can reduce irritation from cold air in some people, but there is no evidence that extra layers or heating will materially shorten a postinfectious cough.

## 5. Behavioural cough suppression

**Evidence confidence: moderate-high for chronic refractory/unexplained cough; indirect for subacute postinfectious cough.**

Speech-pathology programmes reduce symptoms and cough frequency in chronic refractory cough. A placebo-controlled RCT reported substantial improvement with a programme that included cough suppression, breathing/voice strategies and education (`speech-pathology-rct-2006`). A later multicentre trial and a 2024 meta-analysis also support behavioural cough-suppression approaches (`psalti-rct-2017`, `bcst-meta-2024`).

The low-risk elements are reasonable to borrow during a dry postinfectious cough, while being transparent that this has not been proven to shorten the postinfectious syndrome itself.

### Practical cough-control sequence

At the first throat tickle/urge:

1. Avoid taking a large preparatory gasp if possible.
2. Close the mouth and **swallow once or twice**.
3. Take a small sip of water or suck a plain lozenge/hard sweet.
4. Breathe gently through the nose.
5. Use a slow, longer exhalation through lightly pursed lips for 30–60 seconds.
6. If coughing is unavoidable, aim for one gentle, purposeful cough rather than a long series of hard coughs or repeated throat clearing.

This technique is intended for a **dry/irritable cough**. Do not suppress coughing that is needed to clear significant sputum, blood or an aspirated object.

### Can coughing perpetuate coughing?

In cough hypersensitivity/laryngeal irritation, repeated forceful coughing and throat clearing can mechanically irritate the larynx and reinforce the urge-to-cough cycle. Behavioural therapy is partly designed to interrupt that cycle. This mechanism is better established in chronic hypersensitivity cough than in ordinary short postviral cough.

## 6. Nasal and upper-airway treatments

### Saline nasal spray/irrigation

- **Best use:** nasal congestion, discharge, postnasal sensation, allergic-rhinitis pattern.
- **Evidence:** may improve rhinitis symptoms; direct cough evidence is weak/indirect.
- **Harms:** usually minor irritation or occasional nose/ear discomfort; irrigation should use appropriately prepared sterile/distilled/previously boiled and cooled water according to product/public-health instructions.

### Intranasal corticosteroids

- **Best use:** clear allergic rhinitis or chronic rhinosinusitis phenotype.
- **Evidence:** effective for nasal inflammatory disease; CICADA rates cough-specific evidence as weak.
- **Onset:** nasal benefit builds over days rather than acting as an instant cough suppressant.
- **Harms:** local dryness/irritation and epistaxis; technique matters.
- **Practical position:** do not add simply because a cough exists; consider with pharmacist/GP when nasal symptoms support it.

### Antihistamines

- Newer, less-sedating antihistamines are appropriate for allergic rhinitis when allergy symptoms are present, but are not general antitussives.
- Older sedating antihistamines appear in historical upper-airway cough algorithms but can cause sedation, dry mouth and anticholinergic effects. Routine use for an isolated postviral cough is not supported.

### Decongestants

Oral/topical decongestants may relieve nasal obstruction but are not cough treatments. They can cause insomnia, palpitations or blood-pressure effects; topical agents can cause rebound congestion if overused. Use should be symptom-specific and label/pharmacist guided.

## 7. Reflux-directed approaches

### Low-risk behavioural trial

When cough is worse after lying down or after evening meals, a reasonable low-risk experiment is:

- finish food at least **3 hours before bedtime**;
- avoid lying flat immediately after eating;
- consider head-of-bed elevation if reflux symptoms are present (`chest-reflux-cough-2016`).

This can be tried without assuming reflux is the diagnosis.

### Antacids and alginates

These can relieve reflux symptoms. Evidence that they cure an isolated cough is limited. Their role is stronger when heartburn/regurgitation are actually present.

### H2 blockers / PPIs

CHEST and CICADA recommend against treating an isolated cough with acid suppression when there is no history/objective evidence of reflux. PPIs are not quick cough suppressants; even in true reflux-cough, cough improvement can take much longer than gastrointestinal symptom relief (`chest-reflux-cough-2016`, `cicada-2024`).

## 8. Cough medicines

### Dextromethorphan

**Australian status:** current Healthdirect listings show pharmacy products containing dextromethorphan for temporary relief of dry/non-productive cough (`healthdirect-dextromethorphan`).

**Effectiveness:** adult acute-cough evidence is mixed and benefits, when present, are small/short term. It has not been shown to shorten postinfectious cough. It may be considered for occasional symptom suppression (for example, sleep-disrupting dry cough) after a pharmacist checks suitability.

**Important safety:** dextromethorphan has serotonergic activity and can contribute to serotonin toxicity with some antidepressants/other serotonergic medicines; MAOI combinations are particularly concerning. Product-specific contraindications and interactions should be checked with a pharmacist. Sedation/dizziness and gastrointestinal effects can occur.

### Codeine and opioid suppressants

Codeine-containing medicines have been **prescription-only in Australia since 1 February 2018** (`tga-codeine`). Trials have not shown reliable benefit for ordinary acute cough, and opioid adverse effects include sedation, constipation, respiratory depression and dependence. It is not a sensible routine treatment for uncomplicated postinfectious cough.

Australian CICADA recommends against routine opioid use for chronic cough because of long-term risk/limitations (`cicada-2024`).

### Pholcodine

**Do not use.** The TGA cancelled/recalled pholcodine-containing medicines because of the association with potentially life-threatening anaphylaxis to neuromuscular blocking agents used in general anaesthesia. The TGA safety notice was updated in February 2026 and asks clinicians to check for pholcodine use in the prior 12 months before relevant anaesthesia (`tga-pholcodine-2026`).

### Benzonatate

No routine current Australian product was identified in the sources searched. Because absence from a general web search is not definitive, Australian availability should be verified directly against the ARTG before publication. It should not be presented as a standard Australian self-care option.

### Newer specialist antitussives

Australian CICADA 2024 noted emerging cough-specific antitussive agents but stated none were then approved for use in Australia (`cicada-2024`). This requires regulator re-check before publication because approvals can change. These drugs are aimed at refractory **chronic** cough, not an ordinary several-week postinfectious cough.

## 9. Inhalers and airway medicines

### Salbutamol / beta2 agonists

Bronchodilators are not supported as routine treatment for an undifferentiated postinfectious dry cough. They become relevant when wheeze, variable airflow obstruction or an asthma phenotype is present. Potential adverse effects include tremor and palpitations.

### Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)

The 2024 postinfectious review found no convincing benefit from routine ICS for undifferentiated postinfectious cough (`cmaj-postinfectious-2024`).

ICS can be appropriate when cough is due to asthma/eosinophilic airway disease. In persistent/chronic cough, objective assessment (spirometry ± FeNO/bronchial provocation) is preferable to indefinite blind treatment (`cicada-2024`, `asthma-handbook-diagnosis`).

### ICS/LABA combinations

Not a generic cough treatment. Their role is in diagnosed airway disease according to asthma/COPD guidance, not in otherwise uncomplicated postviral cough.

### Ipratropium inhaler

Older ACCP guidance suggested inhaled ipratropium may help some postinfectious coughs (`chest-postinfectious-2006`). Newer systematic-review summaries have not established a clear pharmacologic benefit overall (`cmaj-postinfectious-2024`). This should therefore be treated as **older/low-certainty evidence**, not a routine first-line recommendation.

### Oral corticosteroids

Not appropriate as routine self-treatment for postinfectious cough. Older algorithms reserved them for selected severe cases after other causes were considered. Potential systemic harms make clinician assessment essential.

## 10. Antibiotics

Antibiotics do not treat an ordinary postviral cough and should not be used simply because cough duration is frustrating. Exceptions require a **specific bacterial diagnosis**, such as selected bacterial sinus disease or early confirmed/suspected pertussis (`chest-postinfectious-2006`).

## 11. Ranked intervention framework

This ranking is for a typical adult with a dry, non-worsening, post-infection cough and no red flags. It is a research synthesis, not an individual prescription.

## 12. A rational staged self-care experiment

To learn something rather than changing five variables at once:

### Days 1–3

- Keep indoor RH around 30–50%; if using a humidifier without a demonstrated need, pause it.
- Remove fragrance/essential-oil diffusion and avoid smoke/aerosol irritants.
- Use normal hydration and honey/lozenges only for comfort, not escalating use.
- Practise cough suppression at the first tickle.
- Finish evening food at least 3 hours before bed.
- Record morning and night cough severity (0–10) and sleep awakenings.

### Days 4–7

- Continue if improving.
- If there are nasal symptoms, add saline and consider pharmacist/GP-guided rhinitis treatment rather than adding multiple cough syrups.
- If there are clear reflux symptoms, discuss symptom-directed antacid/alginate or other reflux treatment with a pharmacist/GP.

### If not improving

A cough already persisting for several weeks merits primary-care review if it is not clearly trending down, even without red flags. If it reaches **8 weeks**, chronic-cough evaluation including chest X-ray and spirometry is recommended in Australian guidance (`cicada-2024`).

## 13. GP/clinician work-up

### Before 8 weeks

Earlier review is reasonable when cough is severe, sleep-disrupting, recurrent, diagnostically unclear, associated with pertussis features, or simply not improving after several weeks. The clinician can examine the chest and upper airway, review medicines/exposures, and decide whether early testing is warranted.

### At >8 weeks

CICADA recommends:

- focused history and examination for red flags and triggers;
- **chest X-ray**;
- **spirometry**, usually including bronchodilator assessment;
- cause-directed evaluation for asthma/cough-variant asthma/eosinophilic bronchitis, upper-airway disease, reflux, environmental exposure and laryngeal/cough hypersensitivity (`cicada-2024`).

When asthma remains plausible despite nondiagnostic spirometry, Australian Asthma Handbook guidance supports FeNO, repeated spirometry and, when appropriate, bronchial provocation (`asthma-handbook-diagnosis`).

### Specialist referral

Respiratory or specialist cough-clinic referral is appropriate for refractory/unexplained chronic cough, abnormal investigations, red flags or when diagnosis remains uncertain (`cicada-2024`). ENT/speech pathology becomes particularly relevant when laryngeal symptoms, voice change, inducible laryngeal obstruction or cough hypersensitivity are prominent.

## 14. Red flags

Seek prompt clinical assessment for new or significant:

- coughing blood;
- substantial shortness of breath, especially at rest/night;
- persistent fever/systemic illness or unexplained weight loss;
- swallowing difficulty;
- hoarseness/voice change that persists;
- recurrent pneumonia;
- abnormal respiratory examination;
- substantial persistent productive cough;
- a new/changed cough in a person with significant smoking/vaping exposure;
- deterioration rather than gradual improvement (`cicada-2024`).

Urgent/emergency care is appropriate for severe breathing difficulty, blue/grey colour, collapse, severe chest pain or other acute emergency features.

## 15. Overall conclusion

The evidence supports a **cause-directed, low-intervention approach** to an uncomplicated several-week dry cough after infection. The strongest immediate self-management priorities are to avoid perpetuating irritation, avoid unnecessary excess humidity/airborne irritants, use simple comfort measures, and employ low-risk cough-control techniques. Adding antibiotics, inhalers, PPIs or multiple OTC cough medicines without a matching clinical phenotype is unlikely to improve outcomes and can add harms or obscure what is driving the cough.

If a cough continues beyond the expected 3–8 week postinfectious window, the strategy should shift from symptom suppression to structured chronic-cough investigation.
